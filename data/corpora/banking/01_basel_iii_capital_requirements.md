# Basel III Capital Requirements

Basel III is the international framework for bank capital agreed by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision after the 2007-2009 financial crisis. Its purpose is to make sure that banks hold enough loss-absorbing capital to survive severe losses without taxpayer support. In the European Union the framework is implemented through the Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) and the Capital Requirements Directive (CRD).

## Risk-Weighted Assets

Capital requirements are expressed as a percentage of risk-weighted assets (RWA). Each exposure is multiplied by a risk weight that reflects how risky it is: cash and high-quality government bonds may carry a weight of zero, while unsecured corporate loans carry much higher weights. Banks calculate RWA for credit risk, market risk and operational risk, using either standardised approaches set by the regulator or, with supervisory approval, internal models.

## Tiers of Capital

- **Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)** is the highest-quality capital: ordinary shares and retained earnings, after deductions such as goodwill and certain deferred tax assets. It absorbs losses while the bank continues operating.
- **Additional Tier 1 (AT1)** consists of instruments such as perpetual contingent convertible bonds that are written down or converted into equity when capital falls below a trigger.
- **Tier 2** capital includes subordinated debt with an original maturity of at least five years. It absorbs losses mainly when a bank is being wound down.

## Minimum Ratios

The Pillar 1 minimums are a CET1 ratio of 4.5% of RWA, a Tier 1 ratio of 6% and a total capital ratio of 8%. On top of these minimums banks must hold buffers made of CET1 capital:

- the **capital conservation buffer** of 2.5%, which restricts dividends and bonuses when a bank dips into it;
- the **countercyclical capital buffer**, set by national authorities between 0% and 2.5% to build resilience when credit grows quickly;
- **systemic buffers** for globally or domestically systemically important banks.

Supervisors can also impose bank-specific Pillar 2 requirements after their annual review of each institution.

## Leverage Ratio

Because risk weights can be wrong, Basel III adds a simple backstop: the leverage ratio, defined as Tier 1 capital divided by total exposure (on- and off-balance-sheet exposures without risk weighting). The minimum is 3%. It limits how far a bank can grow its balance sheet with assets that models consider low risk.

## Why It Matters

Higher and better-quality capital lowers the probability that a bank fails and reduces the cost to the economy when it does. Critics argue that higher requirements can raise the cost of lending, which is why the framework was phased in over several years and refined in later reforms that limit how much internal models may reduce RWA compared with the standardised approach.
