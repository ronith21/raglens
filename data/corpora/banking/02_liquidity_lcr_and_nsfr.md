# Liquidity Regulation: LCR and NSFR

Capital protects a bank against losses, but a solvent bank can still fail if it cannot pay its obligations when they fall due. Several banks in the 2008 crisis ran out of cash because wholesale funding dried up overnight. Basel III therefore introduced two liquidity standards.

## Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)

The Liquidity Coverage Ratio requires a bank to hold enough high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) to survive a 30-day stress scenario:

LCR = stock of HQLA / total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days

The ratio must be at least 100%. The stress scenario assumes, for example, that a share of retail deposits is withdrawn, that unsecured wholesale funding is not rolled over, and that committed credit lines are drawn.

High-quality liquid assets are divided into levels:

- **Level 1** assets, such as cash, central bank reserves and high-quality sovereign bonds, count in full with no haircut.
- **Level 2A** assets, such as certain covered bonds and corporate bonds, receive a haircut of 15%.
- **Level 2B** assets, such as some equities and lower-rated corporate bonds, receive larger haircuts of 25% to 50%.

Level 2 assets are capped as a share of the total stock, so that the buffer is dominated by the most liquid instruments.

## Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)

The LCR covers a short, acute stress. The Net Stable Funding Ratio addresses structural funding over a one-year horizon:

NSFR = available stable funding / required stable funding

The ratio must also be at least 100%. Available stable funding gives more credit to stable sources such as capital, long-term debt and retail deposits than to short-term wholesale funding. Required stable funding depends on the liquidity of the assets: long-term loans need more stable funding than liquid securities. The NSFR discourages banks from funding long-term lending with very short-term borrowing.

## Supervision and Reporting

Banks report their liquidity ratios to supervisors regularly and run internal liquidity stress tests through the Internal Liquidity Adequacy Assessment Process (ILAAP). A contingency funding plan sets out how the bank would raise cash in a crisis, for example by using central bank facilities or selling assets from the liquidity buffer.

## Trade-offs

Holding liquid assets is costly because they earn less than loans. Treasury teams therefore manage the liquidity buffer to meet the ratios with a margin while limiting the drag on profitability.
