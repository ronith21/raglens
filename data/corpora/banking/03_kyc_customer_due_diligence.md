# Know Your Customer and Customer Due Diligence

Know Your Customer (KYC) is the set of processes a bank uses to establish who its customers are and whether a business relationship poses a risk of money laundering or terrorist financing. In the European Union the obligations come from the Anti-Money Laundering Directives and, increasingly, from the directly applicable EU anti-money laundering regulation.

## Customer Due Diligence (CDD)

Standard customer due diligence has four main elements:

1. **Identify and verify the customer** using reliable, independent documents or data, such as a passport or an electronic identification scheme.
2. **Identify the beneficial owner** of a company: the natural person who ultimately owns or controls it. In the EU an ownership or voting share of more than 25% is a common indicator of beneficial ownership. Banks consult central beneficial ownership registers but must not rely on them alone.
3. **Understand the purpose and intended nature** of the business relationship, including expected transaction volumes.
4. **Conduct ongoing monitoring** of transactions and keep customer information up to date.

## Risk-Based Approach

Not every customer needs the same level of checks. Banks assign each customer a risk rating based on factors such as country of residence, occupation, products used, delivery channel and source of funds.

- **Simplified due diligence** may be applied to demonstrably low-risk situations.
- **Enhanced due diligence (EDD)** is required for higher-risk situations, including politically exposed persons (PEPs), customers linked to high-risk third countries identified by the European Commission, and complex or unusually large transactions. EDD typically involves establishing the source of wealth and source of funds and obtaining senior management approval.

## Periodic Review

Customer files are refreshed on a schedule that depends on risk: high-risk customers may be reviewed every year, low-risk customers less often. Event-driven reviews are triggered by changes such as a new address in a high-risk country or an alert from transaction monitoring.

## Record Keeping

Banks must keep copies of identification documents and transaction records for at least five years after the end of the business relationship, so that authorities can reconstruct transactions during investigations.

## Consequences of Failure

Weak KYC controls have led to large fines and to restrictions on banks' activities. Beyond penalties, poor due diligence exposes a bank to reputational damage and to being used by criminals.
