# Credit Risk and IFRS 9 Expected Credit Losses

Credit risk is the risk that a borrower fails to repay. It is usually the largest risk on a commercial bank's balance sheet.

## Building Blocks

Credit risk is commonly decomposed into three parameters:

- **Probability of Default (PD)**: the likelihood that the borrower defaults within a given horizon, usually one year.
- **Loss Given Default (LGD)**: the share of the exposure that is lost if default occurs, after recoveries from collateral and workout.
- **Exposure at Default (EAD)**: the amount owed at the time of default, including expected drawings on credit lines.

The expected loss of an exposure is the product of the three: EL = PD × LGD × EAD. Banks price loans to cover expected losses and hold capital against unexpected losses.

## IFRS 9 Impairment

IFRS 9, the accounting standard for financial instruments, replaced the older incurred-loss model with a forward-looking expected credit loss (ECL) model. Banks no longer wait for a loss event before recognising impairment.

Exposures are allocated to three stages:

- **Stage 1**: performing exposures without a significant increase in credit risk since origination. The bank recognises 12-month expected credit losses.
- **Stage 2**: exposures with a significant increase in credit risk (SICR) since origination. The bank recognises lifetime expected credit losses.
- **Stage 3**: credit-impaired exposures. Lifetime expected credit losses are recognised, and interest income is calculated on the carrying amount net of the loss allowance.

There is a rebuttable presumption that credit risk has increased significantly when payments are more than 30 days past due, and that default has occurred when payments are more than 90 days past due.

## Forward-Looking Information

ECL must reflect reasonable and supportable forecasts of future economic conditions. Banks therefore run several macroeconomic scenarios, such as a baseline, an upside and a downside scenario, and weight the resulting losses by their probabilities.

## Why Staging Matters

Moving a large portfolio from Stage 1 to Stage 2 can sharply increase provisions, because lifetime losses are much larger than 12-month losses. This makes the SICR criteria and the macroeconomic scenarios some of the most closely reviewed judgements in a bank's financial statements.
